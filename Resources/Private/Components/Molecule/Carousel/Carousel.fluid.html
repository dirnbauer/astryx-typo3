Carousel — a row that scrolls sideways and snaps. The scrolling, the snapping, the drag and the momentum are CSS and the browser; the two arrows are an enhancement on top of a control that already works, and astryx.js disables them at either end so they never lie about what they will do. `data-g-carousel` is therefore emitted unconditionally: a rail with no arrows simply gives the script nothing to wire. The track and the arrows are rendered here, and that is a change from 2.0's first pass. They were the caller's on the grounds that every rail labels its scroll region differently; four rails then wrote the same `ul` by hand, two of them dropped `data-g-carousel-track` in the process and both arrow pairs lost the `data-g-carousel-prev`/`-next` hooks the script looks for — so the arrows rendered, and did nothing. Naming the region is what varies, so that is what the caller passes: `trackLabel` for a phrase of its own, `trackLabelledby` for a heading already on the page. An arrow exists only once it has a name, because a control announced as "button" and nothing else is no way to move through a rail. That is why `previousLabel` and `nextLabel` are what bring the pair into being rather than a boolean beside them. `ariaLabel` decides the element, as it does for Toolbar: a rail of links that moves a visitor around the site is navigation and needs a named `nav` landmark; an unnamed rail of cards is a plain `div`. `start` and `end` ride along as bare classes beside `astryx-carousel-control` because that is where the stylesheet pins the two arrows to the edges of the rail. The control class is not a component root, so the CSS codemod never moved those two to data attributes; IconButton's own `position` says the same thing in the spelling the codemod did reach.
