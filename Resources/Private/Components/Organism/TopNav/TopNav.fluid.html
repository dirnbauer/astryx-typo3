TopNav — the application bar: a row with a start, an optional centre and an end, and whatever the caller puts in each. Not a second SiteHeader, and the difference is worth stating because two components that do the same thing under different names is how a design system rots. SiteHeader is the website's header: it takes the page menu as an array, renders the brand, the desktop navigation and a mobile panel from it, emits the skip link, and knows what a TYPO3 menu item looks like. It is the right answer for a content site, and there is one of it per page. TopNav knows nothing about menus. It is three flex regions and a landmark, filled with TopNavHeading, TopNavItem and menu components by whoever places it — the bar an application shell puts above LayoutContent, where the entries are tools rather than pages. Reach for SiteHeader when the header renders the site's navigation; reach for TopNav when you are assembling a bar out of parts, or when a second bar is needed below the first. The three regions are always rendered, including the empty ones. Under `align="center"` the bar is a three-column grid whose middle column is what keeps the centre optically centred no matter how uneven the two sides are, and a region that disappeared when its slot was empty would take a column with it. Empty flex boxes cost nothing on screen. `ariaLabel` is not optional in practice. Every `nav` on a page is a landmark, and a screen reader lists them by name; a page with a site navigation and an unnamed app bar offers two entries both called "navigation" and no way to tell which is which.
The default slot is the start, because that is the region every bar fills: the heading and the entries beside it. `center` and `end` are named, which means a call that fills either of them has to spell the start as `` too — Fluid stops collecting loose children as the default slot the moment one named fragment appears.
